# Proposal: Blinq as an Onchain Information Market

## Why Blinq Is Not Gambling by Design, and How a Sharia-Compliant Prediction Market Can Be Built

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Prediction markets are often misunderstood because they appear similar to gambling at the surface level: a user takes a view on an uncertain future event and may profit if correct.

However, this framing misses the deeper function of prediction markets.

A well-designed prediction market is not a casino. It is an **information market**.

Its primary function is to aggregate distributed knowledge, beliefs, research, and private conviction into a public price signal. That price becomes a live probability estimate of a future event.

In this sense, Blinq is better understood as:

> **An onchain information market for pricing uncertainty.**

The goal is not to create random games of chance. The goal is to make uncertainty legible, tradable, verifiable, and useful.

This proposal argues that Blinq can be defended as distinct from gambling when it satisfies the following conditions:

- Markets are based on real-world, objectively resolvable events.
- Prices emerge from user-driven market activity, not house-controlled odds.
- The output of the market is useful public information.
- Settlement is transparent and rule-based.
- Contracts are fully collateralized.
- Market creation is curated to avoid harmful or purely recreational speculation.
- The system avoids interest, excessive ambiguity, manipulation, and exploitative mechanics.

The proposal also argues that a Sharia-compliant prediction market is possible if the design avoids the core prohibited elements of Islamic finance:

- **Riba**: interest or usury.
- **Gharar**: excessive uncertainty or contractual ambiguity.
- **Maysir**: gambling or games of chance.

The key conclusion is:

> A curated, non-levered, fully collateralized, transparent prediction market focused on public-interest forecasting and legitimate risk management can be argued to be an information market rather than gambling, and can form the basis for a Sharia-compliant market design.

---

## 2. The Core Misunderstanding

The common criticism is simple:

> “Users are betting on events, therefore it is gambling.”

This is an incomplete argument.

Many legitimate financial markets involve uncertain future outcomes:

- Equity markets depend on future company performance.
- Commodity markets depend on future supply and demand.
- Insurance depends on uncertain loss events.
- Futures markets depend on future prices.
- Options markets depend on future volatility.
- Credit markets depend on default probability.

If uncertainty alone made something gambling, then a large part of modern finance would also be gambling.

The better question is:

> Is the market primarily designed for random entertainment and wealth transfer, or does it serve an economic and informational function?

A prediction market becomes materially different from gambling when its purpose is:

- Forecasting.
- Price discovery.
- Information aggregation.
- Risk transfer.
- Hedging.
- Accountability of claims.
- Public knowledge formation.

---

## 3. What Makes Gambling Different

Gambling is typically characterized by:

- A game of chance or entertainment-first event.
- No meaningful public informational output.
- Odds controlled by the house or operator.
- A structural edge against users.
- Wealth transfer without productive economic value.
- High addiction risk.
- Outcomes that are random, artificial, or recreational.
- Little or no connection to real economic exposure.

Examples include:

- Roulette.
- Slot machines.
- Dice games.
- Pure sports betting for entertainment.
- Random outcome games.
- Casino-style wagering.

The primary output of gambling is private excitement and monetary gain or loss.

It does not produce a useful public signal.

---

## 4. What Makes Prediction Markets Different

Prediction markets are different because they convert beliefs into prices.

A prediction market asks:

> What does the market currently believe is the probability of a future event?

For example:

- If a contract on an event trades at `$0.65`, the market implies roughly a 65% probability of that event occurring.
- If new information arrives, the price updates.
- If participants disagree, they trade.
- If someone believes the market is wrong, they can express that belief with capital.
- The market price becomes a live, public forecast.

This makes the prediction market a mechanism for information discovery.

The public output is not merely who wins or loses money.

The public output is the probability signal itself.

---

## 5. Blinq as an Onchain Information Market

Blinq-style markets are best understood as open markets for event-based information.

They allow people to trade claims about future outcomes. These claims are:

- Event-based.
- Time-bound.
- Objectively resolvable.
- Priced by market participants.
- Settled through transparent rules.

The market price acts as a real-time estimate of collective belief.

This structure makes Blinq closer to an information exchange than a gambling venue.

A casino manufactures games.

An information market prices reality.

---

## 6. Why Onchain Settlement Matters

The onchain structure strengthens the information-market argument.

A properly designed onchain prediction market can provide:

- Transparent settlement.
- Public auditability.
- Fully collateralized contracts.
- Open market history.
- Verifiable liquidity.
- Rule-based redemption.
- Composable data feeds.
- Reduced reliance on opaque operators.
- Permissionless verification of outcomes and balances.

This is materially different from traditional opaque betting venues where the operator controls odds, internal risk, settlement, and user exposure.

Onchain markets allow the system to be inspected, verified, and challenged.

That transparency supports the claim that the system is a market infrastructure layer, not a casino.

---

## 7. Why Financial Risk Does Not Equal Gambling

Risk exists in every meaningful market.

The presence of risk does not automatically make an activity gambling.

The distinction depends on:

- Purpose.
- Structure.
- Contract clarity.
- Economic function.
- Social utility.
- Presence or absence of manipulation.
- Whether the market produces useful information.
- Whether the market helps manage real-world exposure.

A prediction market can be speculative, but speculation alone is not the same as gambling.

Speculation becomes more defensible when it contributes to:

- Liquidity.
- Price discovery.
- Forecasting accuracy.
- Risk distribution.
- Better public understanding of uncertainty.

This is similar to how traders in commodity or financial markets may speculate, but their activity can still contribute to market depth and price discovery.

## 8. The Public-Good Argument

Prediction markets create a public good: a live probability signal.

Traditional information sources have weaknesses:

- Polls are slow.
- Experts can be biased.
- Media narratives are distorted.
- Social media is noisy.
- Surveys are cheap talk.
- Forecasts are rarely financially accountable.

Prediction markets improve this by forcing participants to attach economic cost to belief.

A person who says, “I think this event is likely,” must back that belief with capital.

This makes the signal more credible than casual opinion.

The market does not eliminate bias or error, but it disciplines belief formation.

A prediction market is therefore useful because it makes claims:

- Comparable.
- Tradable.
- Accountable.
- Continuously updated.
- Publicly visible.
- Economically weighted.

## 9. Strongest Framing

The strongest framing is:

> Blinq is an epistemic exchange.

An epistemic exchange is a market where the traded object is not merely financial exposure, but the credibility of future claims.

This makes it different from a casino.

A roulette table produces no knowledge.

A prediction market on inflation, regulation, elections, protocol upgrades, weather, supply chains, or economic indicators can produce useful public information.

The price becomes a compressed signal of collective belief.

That signal can be used by:

- Investors.
- Researchers.
- Journalists.
- Policymakers.
- Businesses.
- Analysts.
- Communities.
- Risk managers.
- Protocols.
- Public institutions.

## 10. The Sharia Question

A Sharia-compliant prediction market requires careful design.

It cannot be assumed that every prediction market is automatically compliant.

Islamic finance is generally concerned with avoiding:

- **Riba**: interest, usury, or guaranteed return on debt.
- **Gharar**: excessive uncertainty, ambiguity, or hidden risk in the contract.
- **Maysir**: gambling, games of chance, or wealth transfer through wagering.

Therefore, the question is:

> Can a prediction market be designed in a way that avoids riba, minimizes gharar, and does not constitute maysir?

The answer is yes, but only under a disciplined design framework.

---

## 11. Avoiding Riba

A basic spot prediction market does not require interest.

There is no need for:

- Interest-bearing deposits.
- Debt-based leverage.
- Guaranteed yield.
- Lending against positions.
- Time-based return on capital.
- Interest paid to liquidity providers.

A Sharia-aligned prediction market should use:

- Fully collateralized positions.
- Non-interest-bearing collateral.
- No margin lending.
- No debt-based leverage.
- No yield generated from interest-bearing instruments.

Under this structure, the riba objection is significantly reduced.

The user is not lending money for guaranteed excess return.

The user is purchasing a fully collateralized event claim with clearly defined settlement terms.

---

## 12. Reducing Gharar

The contract itself must not be ambiguous.

A prediction market can reduce gharar by ensuring:

- Clear market wording.
- Objective resolution criteria.
- Known expiry time.
- Transparent oracle source.
- Fixed payout rule.
- Fully collateralized settlement.
- No hidden operator discretion.
- Public dispute process.
- Clear redemption mechanics.
- No unclear counterparty obligation.

The key distinction is:

> The event outcome is uncertain, but the contract terms are not uncertain.

That distinction is important.

Commercial contracts often involve uncertain future outcomes. The Sharia issue becomes more serious when the contract itself is vague, deceptive, hidden, or structurally unfair.

A prediction market can be designed so that the obligations are completely clear from the beginning.

Example of clear market design:

> “Will BTC close above $100,000 on Coinbase at 00:00 UTC on 31 December 2026?”

This market has:

- A defined asset.
- A defined price source.
- A defined threshold.
- A defined timestamp.
- A defined settlement condition.
- A defined payout rule.

This reduces contractual ambiguity.

---

## 13. Addressing Maysir

Maysir is the hardest objection.

A prediction market becomes gambling-like when it is used for:

- Pure entertainment.
- Addiction-driven speculation.
- Random events.
- Sports-style betting.
- Celebrity gossip.
- Harmful outcomes.
- Markets with no informational or economic value.
- Markets where users only seek thrill-based payoff.

Therefore, a Sharia-compliant prediction market must not be an open-ended betting platform.

It must be a curated information market.

The difference is not only technical. It is also moral and product-level.

The market must be designed around:

- Public benefit.
- Useful forecasting.
- Legitimate risk management.
- Transparent information discovery.
- Research-backed participation.
- Exclusion of harmful or frivolous markets.
- Controls against manipulation and addiction.

The pro-Sharia argument is strongest when the platform is not positioned as “bet on anything.”

It should be positioned as:

> A market for pricing socially useful uncertainty.

## 15. Market Categories That Should Be Excluded

A Sharia-aligned information market should avoid:

- Sports betting.
- Casino-style games.
- Random number outcomes.
- Celebrity gossip markets.
- Death or assassination markets.
- War escalation markets that create perverse incentives.
- Markets involving private individual harm.
- Markets based on unlawful activity.
- Markets where participants can directly cause the outcome.
- Markets with unclear or subjective resolution.
- Pure entertainment wagers.
- Highly addictive short-duration markets.
- Markets that encourage compulsive trading.

This exclusion framework is essential.

Without curation, the Sharia argument becomes much weaker.

## 16. Hedging and Risk Management

The strongest economic defense of prediction markets is risk management.

A participant may have real-world exposure to an event and use a prediction market to hedge that exposure.

Examples:

- A farmer hedges rainfall risk.
- A logistics company hedges port closure risk.
- A business hedges regulatory risk.
- A protocol hedges governance risk.
- An investor hedges election-related policy risk.
- A solar company hedges weather risk.
- A commodity user hedges supply disruption risk.

In these cases, the market is not merely a recreational wager.

It becomes a tool for managing uncertainty.

This makes the market closer to insurance, futures, or risk-transfer infrastructure than gambling.

A Sharia-compliant design should prioritize markets where hedging and planning use cases are credible.

## 17. Product Design Principles for Sharia Alignment

A Sharia-aligned prediction market should follow these design principles:

### 17.1 No Interest

- No interest on deposits.
- No interest-bearing treasury strategy.
- No lending-based leverage.
- No guaranteed yield.

### 17.2 No Leverage

- Spot-only markets.
- Fully funded positions.
- No margin.
- No liquidation engine.
- No debt extension.

### 17.3 Full Collateralization

- Every contract must be backed by collateral.
- No fractional reserve.
- No hidden counterparty exposure.

### 17.4 Clear Resolution

- Objective event definition.
- Public resolution source.
- Fixed expiry.
- Transparent dispute rules.

### 17.5 Market Curation

- No sports betting.
- No random games.
- No harmful markets.
- No private-person misfortune markets.
- No manipulation-prone markets.

### 17.6 Public-Benefit Focus

- Markets should produce useful forecasts.
- Markets should help with planning, research, or hedging.
- Markets should not exist only for entertainment.

### 17.7 Manipulation Controls

- Insider trading rules.
- Position limits.
- Oracle safeguards.
- Market surveillance.
- Dispute resolution.
- Anti-manipulation enforcement.

### 17.8 Responsible Access

- Cooling-off periods.
- User limits.
- Risk warnings.
- No addictive product loops.
- No casino-style UI.
- No gamified loss chasing.
- No misleading payout framing.

### 17.9 Scholar and Governance Review

- Sharia review process.
- Market admissibility committee.
- Transparent governance standards.
- Periodic compliance review.

## 18. Why “All Prediction Markets Are Halal” Is Not the Right Claim

The correct claim is not:

> All prediction markets are Sharia-compliant.

That would be too broad and easy to attack.

The stronger claim is:

> The prediction market primitive can be made Sharia-compliant when designed as a curated, non-interest, non-levered, fully collateralized information market focused on public-benefit forecasting and legitimate risk management.

This framing is more precise.

It admits that bad implementations can become gambling-like.

But it defends the underlying primitive as valid.

---

## 19. Why “All Prediction Markets Are Gambling” Is Also Wrong

The opposite claim is also too broad.

Saying all prediction markets are gambling ignores their informational and economic function.

A prediction market can:

- Produce real-time public forecasts.
- Aggregate distributed knowledge.
- Improve planning.
- Support hedging.
- Discipline narratives.
- Make uncertainty measurable.
- Reveal market expectations.
- Create transparent probability signals.

These functions are not present in ordinary gambling.

Therefore, prediction markets should be judged by design, purpose, market category, and user protections — not by superficial resemblance to betting.

## 21. Debate Argument in Favor

Prediction markets are often misunderstood because they appear similar to gambling: a user takes a position on an uncertain future event and may profit if correct.

But uncertainty alone does not make something gambling.

Financial markets, insurance markets, commodity markets, and risk markets all involve uncertainty. The real distinction lies in purpose and structure.

A casino creates artificial games of chance where the primary output is entertainment and wealth transfer. A prediction market creates a public price signal about real-world uncertainty.

That price signal is valuable.

It tells the world what informed participants collectively believe about the probability of an event. Unlike polls, pundit commentary, or social media sentiment, prediction markets require participants to put capital behind their beliefs. This makes claims more accountable.

A prediction market therefore functions as an information engine.

It aggregates dispersed knowledge, updates in real time, and produces a transparent public probability.

Onchain prediction markets strengthen this model further because they allow transparent settlement, auditable collateral, public market history, and rule-based redemption.

From a Sharia perspective, the same distinction matters.

A prediction market is not automatically permissible, but it can be designed to avoid the core prohibited elements.

It can avoid riba by eliminating interest, debt, and guaranteed yield.

It can reduce gharar by making every market’s wording, expiry, oracle, payout, and settlement rule explicit.

It can address maysir by excluding recreational betting, harmful events, sports gambling, random games, and addictive product mechanics.

Under this design, the market is no longer a casino-like venue. It becomes a curated information market for useful forecasting and legitimate risk management.

The valid position is therefore:

> A prediction market is not gambling by necessity. It becomes gambling-like when designed for entertainment, addiction, randomness, or harmful speculation. But when designed as a transparent, fully collateralized, non-interest-based, curated market for public-interest forecasting and risk management, it is better understood as an information market — and can form the basis for a Sharia-compliant financial primitive.

## 23. Final Conclusion

Blinq-style prediction markets should not be dismissed as gambling simply because they involve uncertain future outcomes.

The better interpretation is that they are markets for pricing uncertainty.

Their core output is information.

When designed properly, they produce public probability signals, aggregate knowledge, support planning, and enable risk management.

From a Sharia perspective, the primitive can be made more defensible through disciplined design:

- remove interest,
- avoid leverage,
- fully collateralize positions,
- reduce contractual ambiguity,
- curate market categories,
- prohibit harmful and recreational markets,
- focus on public-benefit forecasting,
- implement responsible access,
- and establish Sharia review.

Therefore, the strongest position is:

> A prediction market is not gambling by default. It is an information market by primitive. With the right constraints, Blinq can be designed as a Sharia-aligned market infrastructure layer for forecasting and risk management.
